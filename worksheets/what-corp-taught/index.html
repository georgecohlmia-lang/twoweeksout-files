The Mindset Shift

Before we touch a single number, let's take an honest inventory of how you've been thinking about money — and decide which corporate habits to carry forward and which to set down.

How to use this worksheet: Work through each section in order. Your answers save automatically. There are no wrong answers — only honest ones.
Part 1

The Assumptions You Can't See

The biggest obstacle in this transition isn't what you don't know. It's what you think you know. Start by surfacing three financial assumptions you're carrying from your corporate career — not the obvious ones, the invisible ones.

Assumption #1
Think about timeline, support systems, or resources you've taken for granted. What's one assumption you're quietly making about how things will work?
Assumption #2
What's another one — maybe about the team you'll have, the budget you'll work with, or the runway you can give the business?
Assumption #3
One more. What's a belief about how customers, vendors, or partners will treat you that comes from being inside a known corporate brand?
Part 2

The Four Corporate Habits

There are four habits that tend to carry over from corporate and quietly mislead founders in year one. For each one, rate how strong this habit is in you — and write down where you see it showing up in your thinking right now.

1
Annual Budget Cycles
In a startup, your pricing, revenue timing, and cost assumptions will change materially within ninety days. Anchoring to an annual budget either makes you spend regardless of reality or feel like a failure every month the actuals diverge.
How strong is this habit in you?
2
Timeline & Dependency Assumptions
In corporate, you plan around other teams doing their part. In a startup, those dependencies don't exist. Timelines that felt conservative inside a corporation are almost always optimistic in startup — because they were built on infrastructure you no longer have.
How strong is this habit in you?
3
Resources On Demand
In corporate, the constraint is approval. In a startup, the constraint is existence. Every dollar comes from your capital, your revenue, or your credit. The resourcefulness muscle you didn't have to build before is the one your startup will demand from you on day one.
How strong is this habit in you?
4
Predictable Payroll
In a startup, your draw is tied to cash that may not have arrived. And when you're responsible for making payroll for other people, the weight of that shifts in ways no corporate role prepares you for.
How strong is this habit in you?
Part 3

Keep It or Set It Down

The financial skills you built in corporate transfer. The financial assumptions don't. Go through the list and decide which to bring with you and which to set down before they cost you.

Part 4

Your New Sounding Board

In corporate, there's always someone to lean on — a mentor, a finance team, a supervisor. In a startup, that infrastructure is gone. Decisions get made by you, alone, with incomplete information. So who replaces that?

Who are the two or three people you'll genuinely turn to when you're uncertain?
Not who you should turn to — who you actually will. A peer founder, an old boss, a mentor, your spouse. Be specific. Names.
What's the question you most need a sounding board for in the next 90 days?
The one you've been avoiding because you don't fully trust your own answer.
Part 5

Expecting the Divergence

Revenue will take longer than you projected. Costs will appear that you didn't anticipate. Timelines will slip. That's not failure — it's the normal shape of an early-stage business. The founders who navigate it well are the ones who expected the divergence.

What's one specific area where you're most likely to confuse "normal divergence" with "failure"?
Naming it now makes it less likely to derail you when it happens.
When that divergence happens, what will you do instead of panicking?
A small ritual or rule you can commit to now.
Your Assignment

Two Things Before You Move On

Pick the most important assumption you wrote down in Part 1 — the one that, if it's wrong, would hurt the most. Write what it would cost you in year one. One sentence. That exercise alone will sharpen your planning more than any spreadsheet.

Copy it from Part 1, or write a fresh one.
