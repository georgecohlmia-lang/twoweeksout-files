STEP 01

Write the claim as a sentence somebody could disagree with.

Not a goal. A claim, with a subject and, where possible, a number. “Our existing members would pay a meaningful annual premium for a structured longevity program” is a claim. “Launch a longevity offering” isn’t.

Get the full version out of your head. You’ll cut it in step two.

Which box does it live in? One claim, one box.
Who’ll buyCustomer segments
How much they’ll payRevenue streams
Whether you can deliver itKey activities and key resources

If one box rests on another, say so. That’s different from testing both.

Park them here. One test can’t settle four. Each one gets its own test later.

STEP 02

Make all three cuts. Protect the claim, not the scope.

Most owners only cut scope. The best tests cut all three at once. Write what’s left, and write what you’re leaving out on purpose.

CUT 1Scope

Fewer components, fewer promises, and no new systems or hires. The people who already work here run it by hand.

Only what the claim needs.

Not cancelled. Not part of this test.

CUT 2Audience

Twelve of the right people settle a claim faster than two hundred of the wrong ones. You’re not measuring how many. You’re finding out whether anyone will, and why. Right for what depends on the claim.

Your own list is the right place. The claim is that they’ll do something new, and only they can answer it.
Your own list will fool you. They say yes because they like you. You need people with no reason to be kind to you. One stranger saying yes at full price tells you more than ten warm yeses.
CUT 3Duration

Ninety days with a defined end. A test has an end date. A launch doesn’t, and that difference is how a bad idea survives for two years.

ANDThe price

A test at the real price tells you whether people will pay. A test at a discount tells you whether people like a discount.

STEP 03

Write the result that means stop. Before you know the answer.

A number, agreed by everyone with a vote, recorded where you’ll find it later. Once a test is running, everyone wants it to work. Without a number set in advance, six of twelve becomes momentum and three of twelve becomes early days.

The trap: the test that can’t fail.

The check

What result would make you abandon this, and can the test you designed produce it? If nothing could come back that would stop you, you’ve built a rollout with a small number of people in it.
